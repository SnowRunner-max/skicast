# Station corrections, not finer models, fix Mammoth gusts

No source a browser app can reach today is both finer than HRRR's 3 km grid and shown to forecast alpine gusts better. The closest is the NWS National Blend of Models (NBM): a 2.5 km grid, updated hourly, with gusts already bias-corrected against observations, and free and CORS-open through Open-Meteo. It still has no published ridgetop gust verification, and in a one-morning test it ran well above the summit gusts actually measured. Everything that is genuinely finer has a catch. WindNinja (about 100 m) needs a server and forecasts mean wind only. OpenSnow PEAKS 2.0 (about 50 ft points) has no API. Meteomatics' 90 m downscaling is documented only for temperature. The AI models, ai-weather-guy.com's WeatherNext 3 among them, run at 10–25 km and most have no gust field. The most useful evidence points the other way. Over seven days, HRRR's summit gusts ran **8.9 mph too low** at MTH03 but **4–10 mph too high** at McCoy, Sesame and Canyon. That error is systematic, persistent and opposite in sign between summit and base, which is exactly what per-station bias correction plus a short observation-anchored nowcast fixes. The literature puts the typical gain at 20–40%. The recommended path is to replace the HRRR call before it disappears (NBM as the base), drop the false "elevation-corrected" claim for wind, start archiving observations now, add an in-browser per-station correction and 0–6 h nowcast this season, and fit a proper statistical model (MOS/EMOS) after one season of paired data.

> **Time-sensitive, act before 2026-11-30.** Open-Meteo lists `ncep_hrrr_conus`, the model Skicast pins in `src/config.ts`, as **"Discontinued 2026-11-30"**, two weeks from today ([Open-Meteo NOAA API docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/gfs-api)). NWS sources disagree: the RRFS implementation notice says **"HRRR is not being retired"** with RRFS v1 ([NWS SCN 26-48 rev.](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-048_Updated_RRFS_and_REFS_Implementation.aaf.pdf); [RRFS webinar Q&A](https://www.weather.gov/media/wrn/calendar/RRFS_REF-Webinar%26RegistrationFormQANotes.pdf)), and no NWS retirement notice for HRRR was found. Whichever is right, Skicast depends on Open-Meteo's feed, so the call has to change either way. Separately, Open-Meteo's `elevation` parameter **does not change wind or gust**, so the app's "elevation-corrected" wind is not corrected at all.

## HRRR leaves Open-Meteo in two weeks, and the elevation fix never touched wind

The deadline is the most urgent finding because it breaks the app rather than degrading it. Open-Meteo has already made NOAA's new RRFS model its North America default ("best match") and labels HRRR discontinued as of November 30. NAM, retired November 1, already returns all nulls ([Open-Meteo docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs)). RRFS v1 and its ensemble REFS became operational on **November 3, 2026**, replacing NAM, HREF, SREF and HiresW ([SCN 26-48](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-048_Updated_RRFS_and_REFS_Implementation.aaf.pdf); [SCN 26-47](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-47_Retirement_of_NAM_SREF_HREF_HiresW_NAM_MOS.pdf)). The conflict probably means Open-Meteo is dropping its own HRRR ingest while NCEP keeps HRRR running for now. That reading is an inference, not something either source states. It matters little for Skicast, which reaches HRRR only through Open-Meteo. It does mean any correction tuned to HRRR will need retraining when the base model changes, which favours a correction that adapts itself over one frozen at a single fit.

The elevation problem is quieter but undermines a claim the app makes on screen. Open-Meteo describes `elevation` as the height used for "statistical downscaling" ([Open-Meteo docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs)). In a direct test at the summit, the default elevation, `elevation=nan` and `elevation=3369` returned **identical gusts** (54.1, 54.8, 49.2, 42.7 mph), while temperature shifted about 2.3 °C ([Open-Meteo forecast API](https://api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast)). The comment in `src/lib/openMeteo.ts` saying the forecast "is corrected for" each point's altitude is true for temperature and false for wind.

The same tests point to a likely cause of the summit deficit. With `elevation=nan`, Open-Meteo reports the real height of the model cell, and the NBM cell nearest the summit sits at **2,998 m, about 370 m below the 3,369 m peak**. The NDFD gridpoint for the summit, REV/57,17, sits at 3,009 m ([api.weather.gov gridpoint](https://api.weather.gov/gridpoints/REV/57,17)). One researcher reported an HRRR "model elevation" of 3,310–3,356 m at the summit. That probably came from a default request, which returns the 90 m terrain height of the requested point rather than the cell's ([Open-Meteo Historical Forecast API](https://historical-forecast-api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast?latitude=37.6306&longitude=-119.0328&start_date=2018-08-01&end_date=2018-08-02&hourly=wind_gusts_10m&models=gfs_hrrr)). If so, the HRRR summit cell is also several hundred metres low and averages a smoothed ridge, which would explain the missing speed-up. Neighbouring NBM cells near the summit differed by about **10 mph** in gust (54 vs 42 mph), so which cell a point lands in matters. Open-Meteo's default `cell_selection=land` looks for a cell of "similar elevation" using a 90 m terrain model, while `nearest` takes the closest cell ([Open-Meteo docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs)). Neither setting has been verified to give better gusts at Mammoth.

### NBM is the best free base, but its edge is calibration, not resolution

NBM is the only free product found that already corrects gusts statistically against stations. It fits model gust distributions to observations from the previous 120 days and maps forecasts onto them (quantile mapping), then weights the input models by their recent error ([AMS 2020](https://ams.confex.com/ams/2020Annual/webprogram/Paper362976.html); [Schnapp, WFO Reno](https://www.weather.gov/media/rev/IWAWS/PDFs/2022/Adam%20Schnapp%20-%20Wind%20Updates%20to%20the%20NBM%20v4.1.pdf)). Its known weakness was the same one Skicast sees. After v4.2, NOAA found a "significant low speed bias" at high winds ([Manikin, NBM v4.2](https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/hmt/hmt_webpages/seminars/wwe/2024/20240215_Manikin_NBMv4.2_updates.pdf)). NBM v5.0, implemented around **April 30, 2026**, added "percentile picking" to push the deterministic wind and gust away from the mean, and webinar notes mention a "slight high bias at lower wind thresholds" ([SCN 26-24](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-24_Updated_NBM_V5.0_aac.pdf); [NBM 5.0 webinar notes](https://www.weather.gov/media/wrn/calendar/NBM5.0Q_A-Webinar-Notes.pdf)). That change plausibly explains why NBM read 39–55 mph against a measured 30–37. The trouble is that NBM is calibrated against airport and valley sites (METAR and the URMA analysis), and URMA wind carries a "very noticeable low bias," so nothing in its training targets an exposed 11,000 ft ridge. NBM has four real advantages: it is updated hourly, it survives the HRRR change, it reads higher at the summit where HRRR reads low, and Open-Meteo archives its past forecasts back to at least 2025-01-01 for backtesting. One morning cannot show whether it beats HRRR. It is the right base on which to put a Mammoth-specific correction.

The NDFD gridpoint is the same blend after WFO Reno forecasters edit it. It arrives in blocky time intervals and updates only a few times a day ([api.weather.gov points](https://api.weather.gov/points/37.6308,-119.0326)). It is best used as a "what NWS expects" line or a high-wind sanity check, not as an hourly trace. RRFS is the natural like-for-like replacement for HRRR at the same 3 km and with a 15-minute product, but it gave the lowest summit gust of any model tested. The published HRRR pattern of low strong-wind and lee-slope gusts was blamed on boundary-layer physics, and finer resolution helped only slightly ([Collins et al., NOAA IR](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/67853); [Fovell et al., Atmosphere 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4433/16/1/16)). A 3 km RRFS has no clear reason to fix it.

### Finer products fail on access, gusts or evidence

WindNinja is the only physically motivated tool that resolves ridgetop speed-up. Its evaluation found well-exposed ridgetops "benefitted from downscaling when the stations had negative input HRRR forecast biases," which describes the Mammoth summit. It made lee canyon stations worse, and its skill dropped at higher wind speeds ([Seto et al.](https://ninjastorm.firelab.org/windninja/publications/wefo.pdf)). It produces mean wind, not gusts, and it runs as native software, so the only way to fit it to a no-backend app is a scheduled job that publishes speed-up ratios as static JSON. That is a later experiment, not a first fix. Among the commercial products, OpenSnow PEAKS 2.0 is the one aimed most squarely at this problem: it learns from thousands of stations and adjusts to points about 50 ft across. It offers no API, so Skicast can only link to it. Meteomatics puts a gust variable on its free tier but says its downscaling "does not reveal small-scale weather patterns initially missed" ([Meteomatics downscaling](https://meteomatics.com/en/weather-api/downscaling)). No vendor publishes gust verification in mountains, so a claim that any of them beats corrected NBM at Mammoth can only be settled by logging them against MTH stations ([Schulz & Lerch](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2106.09512) shows how much ranking shifts once local post-processing is applied).

## ai-weather-guy.com shows pictures of a 10 km model with no gusts

The site is not appropriate for Skicast. It is a free, donation-funded map viewer for Google DeepMind's WeatherNext 3 ensemble. It runs no model of its own, and its manifest states "Maps only — no downloadable grids" ([ai-weather-guy.com manifest](https://ai-weather-guy.com/ca/manifest.json)). It has a "Mammoth Mountain" meteogram point, but that point is a single 15-day PNG of the ensemble-mean 10 m and 100 m wind, with no gusts and no numbers ([meteograms manifest](https://ai-weather-guy.com/meteograms/manifest.json)). None of its JSON files sends CORS headers, and every page carries the disclaimer "Not an official forecast" ([ai-weather-guy.com](https://ai-weather-guy.com/)). The model behind it is also a poor fit. WeatherNext 3 provides 10 m wind on a roughly 10 km grid and has **no gust variable** ([Google WeatherNext models guide](https://developers.google.com/weathernext/guides/models)). Getting the data directly means an allowlist request and Google Cloud access, which needs a backend ([WeatherNext access guide](https://developers.google.com/weathernext/guides/access-forecast)). The site's own 90-day airport checks show the ensemble mean running **7.5 mph low at Bishop**, the nearest station to Mammoth, and 4–9 mph low at other western sites ([meteograms scores.json](https://ai-weather-guy.com/meteograms/scores.json)). That is the wrong direction for a summit that HRRR already under-forecasts. The viewer itself is a competent piece of work, apparently by a Desert Research Institute meteorologist judging by the linked GitHub profile ([github.com/ajoros](https://github.com/ajoros)), but it is a synoptic-scale picture, not a lift-point data source.

## The summit error is systematic, flips sign and is fixable from a week of data

A seven-day pairing of each station's hourly maximum gust against the Open-Meteo HRRR gust at the station's own coordinates gives the clearest view of what Skicast actually needs to fix ([api.weather.gov](https://api.weather.gov/stations/MTH03/observations); [Open-Meteo](https://api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast?latitude=37.63059&longitude=-119.03282&hourly=wind_gusts_10m&models=gfs_hrrr&past_days=7)):

| Station | Paired hours | Observed mean gust (mph) | HRRR mean gust (mph) | Bias, obs − HRRR (mph) | Best constant fix | In-sample MAE, raw → fixed (mph) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MTH03 Summit | 152 | 28.2 | 19.3 | **+8.9** | Additive | 9.4 → 5.4 |
| MTH20 McCoy | 156 | 15.4 | 19.4 | −4.0 | Multiplicative | 7.2 → 5.5 |
| MTH05 Sesame | 83 | 16.4 | 26.8 | −10.4 | Multiplicative | 10.6 → 4.2 |
| MTH19 Canyon | 156 | 9.6 | 16.1 | −6.5 | Multiplicative | 7.7 → 3.4 |
| MTH17 Eagle | 0 | no gusts reported in 7 days | – | – | – | – |

The sign flip is the central finding. **HRRR is too low at the exposed summit and too high at sheltered mid and base stations**, matching published HRRR behaviour at windy versus sheltered sites ([Fovell et al. 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4433/16/1/16)). A single mountain-wide offset would make the lodges worse, so every correction has to be per station. These numbers come from one week, are fitted and tested on the same data, and will overstate real-world skill. The literature still supports the direction. Running-bias (Kalman-type) filters are the most effective at removing systematic wind bias in complex terrain ([Plenković et al. 2018](https://ckanprod.data-commons.k8s.ucar.edu/dataset/493e1f68-ee3d-4682-a00c-6996f1226a53)). Analog methods lose their edge "when the errors are strictly systematic" ([Nagarajan et al. 2015](https://harvester.ues.edu.sv/vufind/ProQuestFSGRecord/2827711362)). Station-based gust post-processing cut probabilistic error (CRPS) by **about 29% with simple statistical methods and about 37% with neural networks** across 175 German stations, with mountain sites among the hardest cases ([Schulz & Lerch](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2106.09512)). An out-of-sample improvement of roughly 30–40% at MTH03 is a reasonable target, not a measured result.

The first few hours call for a different tool. The summit's hourly gust error is strongly autocorrelated: 0.73 at a 1 h lag, 0.47 at 3 h and 0.13 at 6 h. That implies a decay time of about 3 h (from −1/ln 0.73), and it matches studies finding that "for horizons less than 1–4 h, persistence is the best model" ([arXiv 2503.18797](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.18797v1)), while observation-anchored methods beat raw NWP out to 6–12 h ([PeakWeather](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.13652); [Kann et al. 2012](https://asr.copernicus.org/articles/8/115/2012/asr-8-115-2012.pdf)). The nowcast is the corrected model plus the latest observed error, decayed as *e₀·exp(−(h + lag)/τ)* with τ ≈ 3 h. The lag term is needed because NWS-delivered gusts arrive about an hour after the latest record, itself about 20 minutes behind, which confirms the 1–2 h gust delay the app already handles.

Three data problems shape the design. First, MTH03's sustained speed and direction are null in about 78% of recent records while gusts still report, so the summit correction must use gusts alone, and a gust-factor method cannot be anchored there. Second, MTH17 Eagle reported no gusts in seven days, and Main Lodge has no station at all, so both need a correction borrowed from a neighbour (MTH19 for Eagle, MTH05 Sesame for Main Lodge) and flagged in the UI. Third, history is shallow. api.weather.gov keeps **exactly 7 days**, the multi-year archive at Synoptic/MesoWest now needs a token, and its free Open Access tier is limited to U.S. `.edu` users and one year of history ([Synoptic Open Access](https://synopticdata.com/pricing/open-access-pricing/)). The Iowa mesonet does not carry the MTH stations ([IEM API](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/api/1/station/MTH03.json)). The model side is easy by comparison. Open-Meteo's Historical Forecast API has HRRR gusts back to 2018 and NBM back to at least 2025, and its Previous Runs API keeps forecasts by lead time from about mid-2024 ([Previous Runs docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/previous-runs-api)). The researchers disagreed on whether the Previous Runs endpoint sends CORS headers: one test saw `*`, the other saw none. Check it from a real browser before relying on it client-side.

## A three-stage plan for Skicast

The ordering follows urgency. The first stage keeps the app working past November 30 and stops it making a false claim. The second captures data that otherwise expires after seven days. The third delivers most of the accuracy gain. The fourth waits for a season of data.

| When | Change | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Now, before 2026-11-30** | In `src/config.ts`, replace `FORECAST_MODEL` `ncep_hrrr_conus` with `ncep_nbm_conus` (label "NOAA NBM"). First run a one-day bake-off over the trailing 7 days of HRRR, NBM, RRFS and NDFD gusts against MTH03, MTH20, MTH05 and MTH19, and switch to RRFS only if NBM is clearly worse after a constant correction | HRRR leaves Open-Meteo on 11-30. NBM is hourly-updated, calibrated, 2.5 km, archived for backtests and unaffected by the change. The bake-off uses data that is free today and expires in 7 days |
| **Now** | Remove "elevation-corrected" from the wind copy and from the `buildForecastUrl` comment. Keep `elevation` for temperature only. Make one diagnostic request per beacon with `elevation=nan` and log the returned latitude, longitude and grid-cell elevation. Check that no two beacons share a cell, and compare `cell_selection=land` with `nearest` | The elevation parameter does not adjust wind. The summit cell is likely about 370 m low, and adjacent cells differ by about 10 mph |
| **Now** | Start an hourly GitHub Action (or Cloudflare Worker cron) that appends all five MTH stations' 5-minute observations, including MTH05, plus each issued NBM, RRFS and NDFD forecast (and HRRR until it stops), to static JSON | api.weather.gov drops data after 7 days and Synoptic history is gated. Every unarchived day is lost for good, and per-lead-time pairs are what later models need |
| **Next, this season** | Add an in-browser, per-station running bias. Use an additive exponentially weighted bias at MTH03, and *obs ≈ a + b·model* or a ratio at McCoy, Sesame and Canyon, with a half-life of about 2–3 days, clamped. Train it from 7 days of NWS observations plus Open-Meteo `past_days=7`, and cache it in sessionStorage. Borrow MTH05 for Main Lodge and MTH19 for Eagle, and say so on screen. Show raw and corrected values side by side | It targets the systematic, sign-flipping error. It needs no new service. It adapts by itself when NBM or RRFS change |
| **Next** | Add the 0–6 h nowcast: corrected model plus the latest gust error, decayed with τ ≈ 3 h from the age of the last gust-bearing record | Persistence dominates the first 1–4 h, and the summit error autocorrelation supports τ ≈ 3 h |
| **Next** | Once the archive job exists, move the correction there and publish per-station coefficients as one static JSON file. Optionally log Meteomatics free (key in job secrets, never in the browser) and Tomorrow.io free alongside NBM. Show the NDFD `windGust` as an "NWS forecast" reference line | Removes about 20 extra API calls per page load. Keeps keys out of client code. Builds the commercial bake-off at zero cost |
| **After one season** | Fit per-station MOS or EMOS (a truncated-logistic gust distribution) on the archive. Predictors: model gust, model speed, sin/cos of direction (ridge-exposure sectors), hour, lead time and a summit-minus-base temperature stability proxy. Publish calibrated quantiles and P(gust above lift-hold thresholds), and keep the running bias on top | Distributional post-processing adds about 25–35% beyond raw. Direction and stability terms capture exposure effects that a constant offset misses |
| **After one season** | Re-rank NBM, RRFS and the logged commercial feeds on held-out data. Optionally test WindNinja speed-up ratios computed by the scheduled job for the summit only, and try gradient boosting or quantile regression forests if two seasons exist | The only credible ranking for Mammoth is a local one. WindNinja helps exposed ridges with low-biased input but hurts lee sites |

Two judgement calls deserve a steelman. One could keep the base model as close to HRRR as possible by choosing RRFS, which is the same 3 km class with 15-minute output. The argument for NBM is that the correction does most of the work, and NBM's hourly refresh, 11-day horizon and archive matter more than its raw summit number. The bake-off exists to overturn that choice if the data disagree. One could also argue the archive job breaks the "no backend" design. It adds no server, only a scheduled job writing static files, and without it Skicast can never get past seven days of training data.

## Conclusion

Looking for a finer model was the wrong question. Every source that improves on 3 km either cannot reach a browser, does not forecast gusts, or has no evidence at a Sierra ridge. HRRR's errors at Mammoth are not mainly about resolution: they are large, persistent, opposite in sign at summit and base,, which a finer grid would not obviously fix. Errors like that are the easiest kind for a small statistical layer to learn, and the measured stations Skicast already reads are the training data. The app's distinctive value can be an INCA-style blend of observations and model at five points, something neither NOAA nor the AI models offer at lift level.

The binding constraints are calendar, not compute. The HRRR feed ends November 30, the NWS keeps observations for only seven days, and multi-year station history has moved behind Synoptic's paywall. Each week without an archive is a week of training pairs gone for good. The decisions that matter most this month are small: swap the base model, drop the false elevation claim, and start saving observations. A statistical model that predicts the probability of lift-hold gusts becomes possible only once a season of data is saved. Two things remain unknown and the plan is designed to measure them rather than assume them: whether NBM's v5.0 high-wind tuning over-forecasts the summit, and who runs the MTH sensors and how they define a gust.
